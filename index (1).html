How money moves between countries

When an Indian company buys machinery from Germany, a student pays fees in Canada, or a foreign fund buys shares in Mumbai, currencies change hands. International finance is the study of those flows, the prices that govern them, and the institutions that keep the system running.

What is international finance?

Domestic finance deals with money inside one country and one currency. International finance adds three complications: different currencies, different governments and laws, and different risks. A firm that sells abroad earns in a foreign currency, so its profit depends on an exchange rate it does not control.

💱

Foreign exchange market

Where currencies are bought and sold. It is decentralised, runs through banks and dealers across time zones, and is the largest financial market in the world by daily turnover.

🌍

International capital flows

Money that crosses borders as investment: foreign direct investment (FDI), portfolio investment in shares and bonds, bank lending and remittances.

🏛️

Global institutions

Bodies such as the IMF, World Bank, BIS and WTO set rules, lend in crises, collect data and settle disputes between countries.

Why it matters to ordinary people

A weaker rupee makes imported fuel, electronics and foreign education more expensive, but helps exporters such as IT services and textile firms. A stronger rupee does the opposite. Interest rates, inflation and growth in the United States can also change how much foreign money flows into India.

Exchange rates

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. If USD/INR is 95, one US dollar costs 95 rupees. When that number rises, the rupee has depreciated; when it falls, the rupee has appreciated.

What moves exchange rates?

📈 Interest rates

Higher returns attract foreign capital, raising demand for the currency.

🛒 Inflation

Persistently higher inflation erodes a currency’s purchasing power over time.

🚢 Trade balance

Countries that import more than they export must sell their own currency to pay for imports.

🧭 Capital flows and sentiment

Risk-off periods push money towards safe assets such as the US dollar, gold and government bonds.

Who gains and who loses when the rupee weakens?

📈 Usually gain

  • Exporters, especially IT and textile firms that earn in dollars
  • Families receiving remittances from abroad
  • Tourism, as India becomes cheaper for visitors

📉 Usually lose

  • Importers of crude oil, gold and electronics
  • Students and travellers paying in foreign currency
  • Companies with dollar loans to repay

Two classic theories

Purchasing power parity (PPP) says exchange rates adjust so the same basket of goods costs the same in both countries. In its relative form:

Expected change in exchange rate ≈ domestic inflation − foreign inflation

Interest rate parity says the forward exchange rate offsets interest differences between two currencies, so investors cannot earn risk-free profit by moving money across borders:

F / S = (1 + idomestic) / (1 + iforeign)

F is the forward rate, S the spot rate, and i the interest rate in each country, with rates quoted as domestic currency per unit of foreign currency.

Balance of payments

The balance of payments (BoP) is a country’s record of all economic transactions with the rest of the world over a period. Because every payment has a matching receipt, the whole account balances. What matters is which part is in surplus and which is in deficit, and how the gap is financed.

AccountWhat it recordsExamples
Current accountTrade and income flowsExports and imports of goods and services, income on investments, remittances from workers abroad
Capital accountSmall capital transfers and non-produced assetsDebt forgiveness, migrants’ transfers, sale of patents or land rights
Financial accountCross-border investment and reservesFDI, portfolio investment, external borrowing, change in foreign exchange reserves

A current account deficit means the country spends more abroad than it earns, so it must be financed by net capital inflows or by drawing down reserves. This is why foreign investor confidence matters so much for countries that import a lot.

Institutions that run the system

After the Second World War, 44 countries met at Bretton Woods in 1944 to design a stable monetary order. That meeting created the IMF and the institution now called the World Bank. Today several bodies share the work.

InstitutionMain jobWhy it matters
International Monetary Fund (IMF)Monitors the global economy, lends to countries with balance of payments problems, gives policy advicePublishes the World Economic Outlook twice a year and manages Special Drawing Rights (SDRs)
World Bank GroupLong-term loans and grants for development and infrastructureFunds projects such as roads, power and education in developing countries
Bank for International Settlements (BIS)Bank for central banks; hosts the Basel Committee on banking supervisionSets the Basel capital standards that large banks follow
World Trade Organization (WTO)Rules for trade between members and a forum to settle trade disputesSuccessor to GATT (1947), established in 1995
Central banks (RBI, Fed, ECB and others)Set interest rates, manage currency and reservesTheir decisions move exchange rates and capital flows worldwide

The global picture in late 2026

The IMF’s October 2026 World Economic Outlook, titled Resilience Under Strain, Urgent Choices, was released at the IMF and World Bank Annual Meetings in Bangkok. It raised the 2026 global growth forecast slightly, to 3.1% from 3.0% in July, down from 3.5% in 2025. Growth is expected to recover to 3.4% in 2027. Heavy investment in AI infrastructure is supporting growth, while energy price increases, Middle East tensions and debt risks weigh on it.

What to take from it

⚖️

Growth is uneven

The IMF describes a split outlook: some economies, such as India, are growing much faster than the world average while others are held back by high energy costs and tighter finances.

🤖

Technology is a financial story too

Large AI investment is lifting growth and stock markets, but the IMF cautions that payoffs are uneven and that a sharp correction could spread across borders through finance.

India in international finance

India is a good case study because it has fast growth, a large services export sector, big oil imports and an exchange rate that the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) manages rather than fixes.

Why does India hold such large reserves?

Reserves give the RBI dollars to sell when the rupee is under pressure, reassure foreign lenders and investors, and cover several months of imports. Reserves fell for several weeks in late 2026, partly because of lower gold prices and RBI intervention in the currency market.

What does “managed float” mean for the rupee?

The market sets the rate day to day, but the RBI steps in to smooth sharp moves, by selling or buying dollars or by changing rules for forex trading. In October 2026 the RBI’s bulletin noted that the rupee gained after measures to curb speculative activity in the forex market.

What drives India’s current account?

India runs a large trade deficit in goods, mainly because of crude oil, gold and electronics imports. It is partly offset by a surplus in services exports (IT, business services) and by remittances from Indians working abroad.
